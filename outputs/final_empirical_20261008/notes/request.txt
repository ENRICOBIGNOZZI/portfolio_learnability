# 2. Scientific objective

The paper is not about finding the best kernel.

It is about the distinction between:

1. **Representation:** which portfolio policies and economic opportunities are available within a model class.
2. **Economic spectrum:** which managed-payoff directions are strongly or weakly identified by historical returns.
3. **Learnability:** how much of that decision space finite market history can reliably support.
4. **Economic implementation:** how much of the learned investment opportunity survives turnover, borrowing costs, and other trading frictions.

The central empirical message should be:

*A rich portfolio representation can make many investment opportunities available. Finite market history determines which directions can be learned. Trading frictions further restrict which learned directions are economically worth exploiting.*

Every major figure must answer a specific economic question.

Do not impose a result before examining the data.

# 4. Critical distinction: the spectral exponent changes in empirical samples

Do not assume that the estimated economic spectrum is stable across time.

At different dates and with different estimation windows, both the estimated eigenvalues and their fitted spectral-decay exponent may change.

In particular, do not mechanically impose the stationary rate

C_T ~ T^(1/(b+1))

on the historical data by pretending that b is fixed across periods.

This theoretical rate applies to a fixed stationary economy satisfying its assumptions. Our empirical exercise is different.

Treat each estimation window as having its own estimated managed-payoff operator and spectrum.

If estimating b_t, treat it as a descriptive finite-sample spectral diagnostic. Do not identify changes in estimated b_t with changes in the population exponent without further evidence.

The primary empirical quantity is:

C_hat(t,T,lambda) = sum_j mu_hat_j(t,T) / (mu_hat_j(t,T) + lambda).

Report relative effective complexity:

**C_hat(t,T,lambda) / T**

where T counts historical monthly managed-payoff observations, not stocks, stock-month observations, or random features.

Report absolute C as a complementary quantity.

In the empirical analysis, C/T may rise, fall, or fluctuate. All three outcomes are admissible.

# 8. Experiment E2 — Economic spectrum and investment opportunities

Keep the kernel-specific managed-payoff spectrum, not the spectrum of the raw characteristics alone.

Construct, for every selected historical sample:

G = the T-by-P managed-payoff feature matrix.

The empirical operator's positive eigenvalues must be computed equivalently from G G' / T or G' G / T.

Do not confuse this with a centered covariance matrix.

Compare Gaussian and Matérn under identical historical cutoffs.

Report spectral concentration, but go one step beyond eigenvalues.

**Determine where the economically useful portfolio signal lies within the spectrum.**

For a fitted policy, use a decomposition into the eigenvectors of its training managed-payoff operator.

Construct nested, progressively activated spectral components:

- strongest directions;
- intermediate directions;
- weaker directions;
- full policy.

Define spectral partitions before evaluating future returns. Do not use future data to choose eigenvectors, order directions, or decide partitions.

Reconstruct the corresponding portfolio policies correctly, including the ridge spectral filter.

Evaluate their incremental OOS economic contribution by comparing the same portfolio objective on the same observations.

A convenient diagnostic is the change in OOS response-one loss when an additional group of directions becomes available.

Do not incorrectly treat Sharpe contributions as additive. Account for cross terms and explain the distinction between incremental portfolio value and standalone eigenportfolio performance.

**Financial question:** Are weakly identified managed-payoff directions economically redundant, or do they contain additional investment opportunities?

Connect the interpretation to Kozak, Nagel, and Santosh.

This is one of the most important potential asset-pricing contributions.
