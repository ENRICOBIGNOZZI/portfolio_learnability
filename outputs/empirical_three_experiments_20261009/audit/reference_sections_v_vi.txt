V. Representation, Learnability, and the Virtue of Complexity

Kelly et al. (2024) study an environment in which expanding the model also captures an increasing
share of the underlying economic signal. In their setting, the improvement in approximation can
dominate the additional estimation cost. This is the virtue of complexity.
   Our question is different. We hold the investor’s information set fixed and vary only the richness
of the portfolio representation. We take the view that a portfolio manager should make available
all information she considers economically useful and can observe in real time. Regularization,
rather than ex ante feature exclusion, should then determine how much of the resulting portfolio
opportunity set can actually be learned from the available data. We therefore study the virtue of
Complexity, as the virtue of richer representation, separately from the addition of new signals.
   Our point in this section is that a richer portfolio representation and a better learned portfolio are
two different objects.

VI. Empirical Portfolio Learnability

We study portfolio learnability using the U.S. monthly stock-level data of Jensen et al. (2023), with
formation dates from January 1963 through December 2024. We follow the characteristic-panel
construction of Didisheim et al. (2024). Starting from the 153 JKP characteristics, we exclude
nano stocks, retain the 130 characteristics with the greatest data coverage, remove stock-months
for which more than 30% of the retained characteristics are missing, and rank each characteristic
cross-sectionally each month to [−0.5, 0.5]. Remaining missing characteristic values are assigned
the neutral value zero. The resulting panel is unbalanced in both the number and identity of available
stocks.
    The initial estimation period covers 1963–1972 and the first validation period covers 1973–1977.
Thereafter, the estimation sample expands, the preceding five years are used for chronological
validation, and the portfolio policy is re-estimated annually. Portfolio weights change monthly with
firm characteristics. The out-of-sample period runs from 1978 through 2024, for a total of 564
monthly portfolio returns.
    We consider three portfolio representations. The Linear specification serves as a finite-
dimensional benchmark. The Gaussian representation allows smooth nonlinear characteristic-to-
weight mappings, while Matérn-3/2 relaxes the strong smoothness restriction of the Gaussian kernel
and admits a broader nonlinear policy class. On the compact characteristic domain,


                                           H𝐺 ⊂ H𝑀,3/2 .


Our main representation comparison is therefore between Gaussian and Matérn-3/2: the latter
makes a larger set of portfolio policies available, but the additional directions must still be learned
from a finite history of returns.
    The nonlinear specifications are implemented using 10,000 Random Fourier Features with a fixed
random draw throughout the backtest. The kernel lengthscale is fixed using the median pairwise

Effective complexity measures the breadth of the managed-portfolio spectrum that remains active
after regularization. A rich representation can make many portfolio directions available, while finite
return history may support only a much smaller subset of them.
   Figures 6 and 7 illustrate this distinction. Within the same final estimation window, we hold
the representation fixed and vary only 𝜆. Relaxing regularization increases effective complexity
by moving progressively deeper into the managed-portfolio spectrum. Historical performance
continues to improve as complexity rises. Subsequent out-of-sample performance, however, reaches
an interior maximum and then declines. In the 2024 test window, the descriptive out-of-sample
Sharpe peak occurs at effective complexity of approximately 66 for Gaussian and 72 for Matérn-3/2.
   This is the central empirical pattern. At low complexity, relaxing regularization allows the
portfolio to exploit additional investment directions and improves subsequent performance. Beyond
an interior point, additional directions continue to improve historical performance but deteriorate
out-of-sample results. The marginal directions made active by lower regularization are increasingly
difficult to learn reliably from the available market history.
   The comparison between Gaussian and Matérn-3/2 gives this result a direct economic interpre-
tation. Matérn-3/2 provides access to a broader set of portfolio policies, but its managed-portfolio
spectrum also contains a longer tail of weaker directions. The value of the larger opportunity set
therefore depends on whether those additional directions can be learned with sufficient precision.
   The empirical evidence separates three objects. Representation determines which investment
opportunities can potentially be expressed. The managed-portfolio spectrum describes the economic
structure of those opportunities. Effective complexity determines how much of that spectrum is
used after regularization. A richer representation can therefore improve the available opportunity
set without implying that all of the additional portfolio complexity should be exploited in finite
samples.
