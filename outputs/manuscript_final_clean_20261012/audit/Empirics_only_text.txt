VI Empirical Portfolio Learnability
A Data, Representation, and Investment Performance
The empirical exercise asks how a representation of stock characteristics, ﬁnite return history, and
regularization jointly shape a portfolio policy. A richer representation makes additional portfolio
opportunities available, but a ﬁnite return history does not necessarily make all those opportunities
learnable or ﬁnancially valuable. We ﬁrst compare Linear, Gaussian, and Matérn- 3/ 2 policies on
identical subsequent months. We then ﬁx the Gaussian representation to examine regularization
and the economic content of its additional directions. The evidence is descriptive historical evidence:
neither a favorable Sharpe nor an estimated number of active directions certiﬁes a reliably proﬁtable
opportunity.
We use the monthly U.S. equity characteristic panel of Jensen, Kelly, and Pedersen, with for-
mations from January 1963 through December 2024. Starting from their research dictionary, we
exclude nano stocks, retain the 130 characteristics with greatest coverage in 1963–1972, and remove
observations missing more than 30% of these inputs. Observed values receive average cross-sectional
ranks on [− 0. 5, 0. 5]; remaining missing inputs receive zero. The panel is unbalanced. The retro-
spectively constructed characteristic dictionary and current data snapshot constitute the academic
information set, rather than a reconstruction of historical signal discovery or data vintages.
There is a material sample limitation. Unavailable subsequent payoﬀs are excluded before ranking
and calculating the number of stocks Nt. A formation-only audit leaves 7,489 unidentiﬁed stock-
month payoﬀs out of 2,504,402 eligible observations (0.299%); documented adjacent-calendar source
matches recover none. Formation-only ranks are invariant to perturbing future payoﬀ availability,
whereas the retained complete-payoﬀ sample is conditional on that availability. Thus selection uses
only earlier observed payoﬀ values, but sample formation still depends on whether future payoﬀs
are available. Unknown returns are never assigned zero. All performance and cost results retain
this caveat.
The ﬁrst inner training period uses 1963–1972 formations and validation uses 1973–1977. Each
subsequent January-close decision reserves the preceding 60 formation months for validation, then
reﬁts on the full available pretest history. December’s payoﬀ is known at January close. January–
December formation positions therefore realize February–next January returns. All three policies
are evaluated on the same 564 OOS months, February 1978–January 2025.
Linear uses a constant and the characteristic coordinates. Gaussian and Matérn- 3/ 2 use 10,000
random Fourier features with seed 0. For each kernel, chronological validation jointly selects length-
scale and ridge penalty by minimizing the mean response-one quadratic loss Q at ﬁtted scale.
Lengthscales are {0. 25, 0. 5, 1, 2, 4} times the initial-sample median distance; each has 120 penalties
ﬁxed from its own initial training spectrum. Frequency and phase draws are shared across length-
scales. The chosen pair is reﬁtted using training and validation observations before the test year;
no test payoﬀ selects either parameter. Linear selects its penalty by the same chronological loss
criterion. This is a comparison of representations under past validation; ﬁnite feature banks do not
establish strict nesting or identify a population approximation gap from an observed performance
ranking.

The evaluation grid retains every original penalty and adds a logarithmic sequence from strong
regularization to very small positive penalties, followed by exactly λ = 0 . Its 1,522 policies are

operator. Direct feature-space least squares checks the ﬁrst and ﬁnal endpoints, and the smallest
positive penalties converge numerically to the zero returns. No added evaluation changes the
original validation policy, and no OOS result selects a deployable penalty.

C What Additional Directions Trade
We interpret portfolios associated with eigenspaces of the same ﬁxed Gaussian policy. At each
annual reﬁt, four disjoint positive-rank groups partition its ridge coeﬃcients. Their actual contri-
butions are reconstructed as stock-level signed holdings, providing long and short characteristic
proﬁles and subsequent payoﬀ and covariance attribution. The same selected penalty is retained
across nested comparisons. These are attribution portfolios within one policy, not independently
optimized strategies. Near-tied eigenvectors can rotate, so group proﬁles carry the historical inter-
pretation rather than persistent names assigned to individual eigenvectors.

Gross Sharpe rises from 3.012 to 3.284, and net Sharpe from 2.354 to 2.598. Paired 12-month-
block intervals for these changes are [0.002,0.546] and [0.011,0.487]. The response-one loss changes

of the previous policy P and added payoﬀ A, at their original ﬁtted scale.
Small incremental account gross does not imply small turnover. Monthly characteristics and
annual reﬁts can change oﬀsetting positions substantially. For every nested account, stock weights
are summed before calculating trades against drift-adjusted holdings, short notional, and net re-
turns. Costs begin with entry from cash. Target weights refer to post-execution NA V; proportional
commissions are solved jointly with the target cash residual. The annual short fee is divided by
twelve. Cash earns or pays the same monthly risk-free rate, and terminal holdings are marked
rather than forcibly liquidated. Separate component fees would not reconstruct the netted account.
Changes in target weights remain a separate turnover proxy, never observed execution costs.
Corporate-action and delisting treatment relies on the upstream JKP return construction and
stable identiﬁers; there is no independent share-count or execution-tape reconstruction. Liquidated
names incur trades but receive no invented next payoﬀ. The cost assumptions omit market impact
and additional funding spreads. They are self-ﬁnancing friction sensitivities, with the same future-
payoﬀ-availability caveat as gross returns.
The weak tail has little historical second-moment mass and small absolute payoﬀ contributions.
The successive gross/net Sharpe increments in Table III include adverse as well as favorable out-
comes; the loss decomposition also retains the own-square and cross terms. Small mass alone
cannot rule out covariance value, while a striking normalized characteristic tilt need not have a
material ﬁnancial contribution. Six-benchmark regressions of the actual component returns give
descriptive spanning statistics; a separate hedge uses only pretest slope estimates. Neither supplies
an independently selected investment strategy or a newly identiﬁed risk premium.
Economic content and supported complexity vary with history. The stationary theory is a bench-
mark for approximately stable conditions; a local interpretation requires those conditions. De-
scriptive complexity changes do not identify a population spectral-decay parameter. Across the
representation comparison, ﬁxed-kernel regularization paths, and stock-level attribution, the ﬁnan-
cial question is whether additional available directions improve the portfolio that ﬁnite historical
information can support.
