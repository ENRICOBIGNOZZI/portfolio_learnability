# CODEX — Final Theory-Guided Regularization Experiment

## Research objective

Work in the repository:

`ENRICOBIGNOZZI/portfolio_learnability`

We have already implemented a theory-guided regularization schedule for the Matérn-3/2 portfolio kernel:

\[
\lambda_T=C_0T^{-\widehat b_T/(\widehat b_T+1)}.
\]

The constant \(C_0\) is calibrated once using initial chronological validation; the empirical spectral exponent \(\widehat b_T\) is subsequently re-estimated at each annual refit.

The existing experiment reports an **annualized gross OOS Sharpe of approximately 3.561** on 564 months.

The annual cross-validation benchmark with fixed lengthscale obtains approximately 3.461. Joint annual cross-validation of lengthscale and regularization obtains approximately 3.587.

These values come from different existing experiments, so reproduce their monthly payoff paths before making comparisons.

**We want to answer two questions:**

1. Does re-estimating \(b_T\) improve portfolio performance compared with fixing \(b=b_0\) at its initial estimate?
2. Can theory-guided regularization become more useful when we optimize kernel lengthscale chronologically while preserving the theoretically motivated lambda schedule?

The aim is a small, clear, finance-relevant result for *The Law of Portfolio Learnability*, not a new independent methodology paper.

Do not rewrite the manuscript, introduce new kernel families, or construct an unrelated algorithm.

# EXPERIMENT 1 — Does updating b actually matter?

## 2. Freeze the baseline representation

Fix the Matérn-3/2 kernel at \(\ell_0\).

Replicate the initial calibration exactly as in `complexity_schedule.py`:

- Initial training: 1963–1972, \(T_{\mathrm{train},0}=120\) months.
- Initial validation: 1973–1977, 60 months.
- Initial 200-candidate ridge grid.
- Estimate \(b_0\) from the initial training spectrum.
- Choose \(C_0\) once using validation squared portfolio loss.
- First portfolio refit on the complete available history of 180 months.

Importantly, use \(T=120\) when converting the initial validation-selected penalty into \(C_0\), exactly as the original implementation does. Use \(T=180\) for the first full-history portfolio refit.

Freeze the calibrated constant.

## 3. Compare two theoretical schedules

### Strategy A — Fixed spectral exponent

Estimate \(b_0\) once on the initial training sample.

For every annual refit, use

\[
\boxed{
\lambda_T^{A}
=
C_0T^{-b_0/(b_0+1)}.
}
\]

Estimate the portfolio policy on the expanding training history.

Neither \(C_0\) nor \(b_0\) changes.

### Strategy B — Updated spectral exponent

This is our existing method:

\[
\boxed{
\lambda_T^{B}
=
C_0T^{-\widehat b_T/(\widehat b_T+1)}.
}
\]

Update \(b_T\) from the currently available managed-payoff spectrum.

Keep \(C_0\) unchanged.

Use exactly the original spectral regression convention:

- Positive eigenvalues of the uncentered managed-payoff second-moment operator.
- Numerical relative threshold \(10^{-12}\).
- Log-eigenvalue against log-rank OLS regression.
- Predefined rank band from 10% to 60% of positive empirical ranks.
- No clipping of invalid \(b\).

Use identical histories, portfolio solvers, features and OOS observations.

**The only difference between A and B must be whether \(b\) is updated.**

## 4. Required results

Calculate annual and cumulative OOS:

- Gross Sharpe.
- Net Sharpe using the established trading/borrowing-cost implementation.
- Mean and volatility.
- Effective complexity.
- Annual lambda.
- Turnover and leverage.
- Maximum drawdown.
- Response-one quadratic loss.

Report paired bootstrap confidence intervals for differences in Sharpe and quadratic loss.

Most importantly, quantify how much of the change in lambda comes from:

1. Increasing training sample size \(T\).
2. Re-estimating \(b_T\).

Show a counterfactual penalty path that holds \(b=b_0\) while using the observed expanding \(T\).

**Decisive question: Does updating b provide a measurable benefit beyond the original theoretical sample-size scaling?**

Do not treat an insignificant difference as proof of equivalence.

# EXPERIMENT 2 — Theory-guided lambda with optimized lengthscale

## 5. Keep the same kernel family

Now allow Matérn lengthscale to vary across the existing five candidates:

\[
\boxed{
\ell\in\ell_0\{0.25,0.5,1,2,4\}.
}
\]

Reuse the existing managed-payoff caches generated by `bandwidth_tuning.py`.

Do not generate different independent RFF draws across lengthscales.

The entire experiment must keep the same return histories, security universe, characteristic rankings, RFF count and feature seed.

## 6. Calibrate the constants once, independently for each lengthscale

For each candidate \(\ell\), use only the original 1963–1972 training observations and 1973–1977 validation observations.

Estimate its initial exponent:

\[
\widehat b_0(\ell).
\]

Select its initial penalty:

\[
\lambda_0^{CV}(\ell)
\]

using the same initial 200-candidate procedure as Experiment 1, but applied to that lengthscale's managed-payoff representation.

Construct the corresponding constant:

\[
\boxed{
C_0(\ell)
=
\lambda_0^{CV}(\ell)
T_{\mathrm{train},0}^{\widehat b_0(\ell)/(\widehat b_0(\ell)+1)}.
}
\]

Once calibrated, all five \(C_0(\ell)\) values remain frozen for every subsequent year.

This is **one initial calibration per representation**, not annual optimization of lambda.

Do not assume one universal \(C_0\) across lengthscales without justification: changing the RKHS changes the relevant source and regularization constants.

Report all five initial exponents, penalties, constants, validation losses and numerical diagnostics.

### Important admissibility check

The paper's polynomial-spectrum theorem assumes \(b>1\).

If a particular initial or subsequent lengthscale produces \(\widehat b\leq1\), do not silently clip the estimate.

Report it as a violation of the assumptions underlying the theoretical plug-in rule. Predeclare how unavailable candidates are handled, preserve the baseline lengthscale as a control, and report whether candidate exclusions materially affect the comparison.

Do not hide failures by replacing them with CV-selected penalties.

---

## 7. Two adaptive-lengthscale strategies

### Strategy C — Optimized lengthscale, fixed b

For each candidate \(\ell\), keep its initial exponent fixed:

\[
\boxed{
\lambda_T^{C}(\ell)
=
C_0(\ell)
T^{-\widehat b_0(\ell)/(\widehat b_0(\ell)+1)}.
}
\]

At each annual decision, select the best candidate lengthscale using chronological validation.

Do not retune lambda.

### Strategy D — Optimized lengthscale, updated b

For each candidate \(\ell\), re-estimate its spectral exponent:

\[
\widehat b_T(\ell).
\]

Then use

\[
\boxed{
\lambda_T^{D}(\ell)
=
C_0(\ell)
T^{-\widehat b_T(\ell)/(\widehat b_T(\ell)+1)}.
}
\]

Again, select only the lengthscale through chronological validation.

**This is our principal proposed algorithm.**

The lengthscale changes the representation. The spectral rule determines lambda.

No annual 120- or 200-point search over lambda is permitted after initial calibration.

---

## 8. Implement lengthscale selection without leakage

Use the existing annual chronology.

For a decision year \(y\):

1. Identify the expanding inner-training observations ending before the five-year validation block.
2. For every candidate lengthscale, compute the candidate's spectral exponent using only inner training.
3. Calculate its theory-guided lambda using that candidate's previously frozen \(C_0(\ell)\) and the inner-training sample size.
4. Fit its portfolio policy using inner training only.
5. Evaluate the five candidate policies on the subsequent 60-month validation period using mean \((1-R^p)^2\).
6. Select the lengthscale with the lowest validation loss, preserving the repository's existing deterministic tie convention.
7. After choosing lengthscale, refit on the entire history available at the decision date.
8. Re-estimate \(b_T\) on that complete past history for Strategy D, or retain \(b_0(\ell)\) for Strategy C.
9. Calculate the final lambda from the theoretical formula with the full refit sample size.
10. Evaluate the following OOS year.

**Never calculate the validation-selected lengthscale using a penalty or spectral exponent fitted on observations belonging to that validation block.**

The final refit may use all information available before the test period, exactly as the existing paper procedure does.

The initial validation period is also used to calibrate \(C_0(\ell)\), so document that this is a historical calibration, not independent evidence of subsequent generalization.

Report primary comparisons from 1979 onward, excluding the anchor year, and a secondary common 1984–2024 comparison to assess early overlap between calibration and repeated validation windows.

# EXPERIMENT 3 — The final controlled comparison

## 9. Compare the full 2 × 2 design

This table is the principal experiment:

| | Fixed lengthscale | Optimized lengthscale |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed initial b | Strategy A | Strategy C |
| Updated b each year | Strategy B | Strategy D |

The design lets us answer four separate questions.

**Q1.** Does updating \(b\) help when lengthscale is fixed?

Compare B against A.

**Q2.** Does optimizing lengthscale help when \(b\) is fixed?

Compare C against A.

**Q3.** Does optimizing lengthscale help when \(b\) is updated?

Compare D against B.

**Q4.** Does updating \(b\) help after allowing lengthscale to vary?

Compare D against C.

Include the interaction:

\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{interaction}}
=
(SR_D-SR_C)-(SR_B-SR_A).
\]

Treat this as a descriptive difference-in-differences between fitted strategies, not as a causal treatment effect.

# EXPERIMENT 4 — Should we move to monthly rolling estimation?

Do not automatically replace the expanding-history annual experiment with a new rolling methodology.

The previous 3.561 benchmark uses expanding windows and annual refits.

A monthly rolling experiment changes both the statistical problem and the amount of information being used, so it must be evaluated separately.

After Experiments 1–3 are complete, perform a lightweight diagnostic on trailing 180- and 240-month managed-payoff windows, moving one month at a time.

Without refitting full portfolios initially, calculate the monthly trajectories of:

\[
\widehat b_{t,W},\qquad
\lambda_{t,W}
=
C_0(W)W^{-\widehat b_{t,W}/(\widehat b_{t,W}+1)},
\]

and their implied effective complexities.

For this diagnostic, explicitly recalibrate or translate the initial constant according to a well-defined pretest window convention. Do not reuse the expanding-history constant without checking that the same parameterization is valid.

Measure:

- Month-to-month variability of estimated b.
- Sensitivity to spectral rank range.
- Frequency of \(\widehat b\leq1\).
- Monthly variation in theoretical lambda.
- Monthly variation in effective complexity.

If the changes are economically negligible or dominated by numerical instability, conclude that monthly re-estimation is not currently justified.

Do not run another expensive full monthly OOS portfolio backtest unless the diagnostic provides a compelling reason.

## Scientific objective

The preferred contribution is not another hyperparameter-tuning algorithm.

It is to test whether the paper's existing theoretical law,

\[
\lambda_T\asymp T^{-b/(b+1)},
\]

can provide a useful and simple portfolio regularization schedule when the representation itself is selected using historical information.

**We want to know whether the statistical law helps us learn better portfolios—not merely whether it generates smoother lambda paths.**

Report the negative results with the same care as the positive ones.
